# Climate Attention Atlas review — 10 September 2026

**Overall assessment: a strong research prototype whose measurement and inference need to catch up with its breadth.** The collection infrastructure and exploratory interface are useful. The present findings justify further investigation, but not claims that disasters cause climate attention, that some countries respond more strongly than others, or that particular fires receive excessive attention.

This review covers the repository, local Parquet metadata, the 5 September frontend snapshot, exported event estimates, UI components and chart definitions, and upstream documentation. I independently recalculated the reported summaries and selected sensitivity checks. All 112 Python tests and 35 frontend tests passed; the production build passed. No connected browser was available, so screen assessments concern implemented content, controls, chart logic, and CSS. Visual rendering, mobile interaction, accessibility, browser regression tests, and the deployed Supabase snapshot were not verified. No analysis code or data was changed.

| Dimension | Rating | Assessment |
|---|---:|---|
| Pipeline and reproducibility | 8/10 | Resumable collection, frozen configurations, cached responses, provenance, typed records and tests are substantial strengths. Serving/export consistency still needs work. |
| Exploratory methodology | 6/10 | External event definition, explicit windows and medians are sensible. Mapping support, small baselines, overlapping events and unvalidated classifiers materially limit interpretation. |
| Strength of substantive evidence | 4/10 | Some interesting associations, but small and changing cohorts, no credible counterfactual, and fragile significance labels. |
| Screen structure and analytical usability | 6/10 | Useful analytical views, but too many overlapping routes and changes in estimand between screens. This is not a visual-design score. |
| Storytelling | 5/10 | The question is good; readers encounter capabilities and selected positive findings before a balanced account of what has been learned. |

**What is already good.** Preserve the separation of GDELT outcomes from the external event catalogue, distinct-URL deduplication within topics, original-language phrase metadata, explicit outlet-country definitions, visible provider outages, complete-window rules, separate GDACS and MODIS measures, medians rather than only means, and the distinction between political volume and political share. IQR is correctly described as variation between events, and the interface often explicitly disclaims causal inference. Most weaknesses below are reasons to strengthen this foundation rather than replace the project.

**What the results actually establish.** These are recalculations from `frontend/public/data/event-study.json` and `event-study-2026.json`, for major floods and wildfires, complete windows, existing overlap exclusion, and non-null percentage changes. They reproduce the current exported implementation, including the defects identified below.

| Onset measure, 14-day window | 2025 median (eligible events) | 2026 median (eligible events) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Affected-market climate URL counts | +9.5% (16) | +27.6% (9) |
| Global climate URL counts | +4.2% (19) | −1.7% (11) |
| Affected-market EV URL counts | +3.4% (16) | −5.8% (9) |
| Global EV URL counts | −7.1% (19) | −1.1% (11) |

The domestic/global rows do not have identical samples, so their difference is not an estimated geographic spillover effect. “Affected” can also pool multiple publishing markets. The two years have different event composition, and 2026 is partial; the larger 2026 median does not establish an increasing trend.

| Affected-market climate onset window | 2025 median (n) | 2026 median (n) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 7 days | 0.0% (20) | +3.0% (13) |
| 14 days | +9.5% (16) | +27.6% (9) |
| 28 days | +1.9% (13) | −5.0% (6) |

This sensitivity mixes changes in timing with changes in cohort. Restricting each year to events usable at all three windows gives 2025 medians of −2.4%, +9.5%, +9.7% (12 events) and 2026 medians of +1.5%, +11.7%, −5.0% (6 events). Show both the available-cohort and fixed-cohort analyses before telling a story about persistence. Post-event persistence is a separate estimand and should remain separate from longer onset windows.

Seven of the 16 eligible 2025 events and four of nine 2026 events have fewer than five baseline climate URLs/day. For example, the 2026 DRC event has a +50% change from a baseline of about 0.14 URLs/day: two URLs in the pre-period become three in the post-period. A headline percentage obscures the amount of evidence.

I also reproduced the overview's exact one-sided 7-day post-event screen: 53 testable major events, 10 nominal p<0.05 flags. A diagnostic Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment at 5%, treating exactly those 53 tests as one family, retains five event flags: Pakistan, Sri Lanka and three Spanish fires. This does **not** validate their inference: temporal exchangeability and event dependence remain unresolved, and the wider collection of browsable hypotheses constitutes a larger search space. The repeated Spanish flags should be investigated as a shared episode, not advertised as independent replications.

For ID 3, allowing smaller-event contamination to remain may be a defensible study choice, but it must be fixed and disclosed. Under the 14-day primary affected-climate specification, 10/16 currently eligible 2025 events and 6/9 2026 events overlap at least one other catalogue event when all tiers are considered. Simply excluding everything may select atypically isolated disasters and leave too little information. Episode clustering or explicit exposure modelling is preferable to a hidden change of rule.

Across screens, CSS and chart ticks repeatedly use 8–10px text, including methodological qualifiers and controls. Increase default readable text and reduce density; verify keyboard navigation, focus handling in the dialog, colour contrast and mobile charts in a connected browser. Share/export should preserve the full specification, cohort counts, dates, source version and a plain-language caveat. A downloaded chart without those details would be easy to misrepresent.

**Recommended information architecture.** Use four main destinations: **Findings**, **Trends**, **Events**, and **Data & Methods**. Findings contains the short narrative and pooled study; Trends combines topic counts, anomalies, event activity and optional physical context; Events contains the map and a full event-detail workspace. The pooled study can expose advanced “Timing”, “Markets”, “Political coverage”, and “Severity” views. Preserve a researcher mode with all specifications, but do not make every visitor configure a model before learning anything.

There is genuine redundancy between Event study and Wildfire attention leaderboards; Event activity and Attention timeline controls/charts; Attention and Coverage breakdown phase comparisons; and Info/Data/Methods descriptions of the same inputs. The distinction worth preserving is **time-series context, event-level evidence, pooled comparison, and measurement audit**. Organizing around these user questions is clearer than organizing around each successive feature added to the repo.

**A stronger narrative would say:** “Some major disasters coincide with increased climate coverage in affected publishing markets. The magnitude varies by event, baseline and observation window; global patterns are mixed, and there is no consistent EV response in the present sample. These associations motivate a more controlled test.” Follow that with a paired-cohort result, the sensitivity grid, and three transparently selected examples: an increase, a flat response and a decrease. For each, show daily evidence, market coverage, physical timing and competing news. A manually reviewed sample should distinguish articles that explicitly connect the event to climate change or EV policy from unrelated topic publishing on the same dates.

The next substantive questions should be: Does the climate-topic share rise beyond normal calendar variation? How quickly does any increase decay? Is it local, international or shared? Does political content change as a share of topic coverage? Do effects vary with population exposure, physical severity, baseline coverage or prior disasters? How often does the article actually connect the event to climate or transport? A paired contrast between climate and EV responses should be estimated directly; “significant for climate but not for EVs” would not establish a difference.

**Recommended sequence.** First fix mapping/missingness, cross-year windows, overlap policy and drawer gaps; rebuild the derived assets and serving tables and publish a change log of affected estimates. At the same time replace misleading verdicts and residual labels. Second, validate taxonomy/country attribution and denominators, define the primary estimand and run placebo/matched-date analyses. Third, publish an honest findings page with fixed-cohort sensitivities, meaningful event examples and a complete downloadable specification. Fourth, simplify navigation and unify the shared analytical components. Extend history after measurement definitions are stable. Search/social sources, more event types and prediction are later priorities: they introduce additional constructs and validation burdens before the current question is resolved.

**Source checks.** GDACS documents media/institutional flood inputs in its [flood methodology](https://www.gdacs.org/Knowledge/models_fl.aspx). Its [wildfire methodology](https://data.gdacs.org/Knowledge/models_wf.aspx) describes size/impact selection, expert alert assignment and GWIS/FIRMS inputs. [SciPy's permutation-test documentation](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.permutation_test.html) specifies the independent-sample random-assignment null that the current day-shuffling procedure requires. NASA's [MOD13 v6.1 user guide](https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/documents/621/MOD13_User_Guide_V61.pdf) describes the vegetation products' QA layers. These documents support the measurement checks; the numerical findings above were recalculated from local project data, not taken from external publications.
